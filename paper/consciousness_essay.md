## Introduction

I have long been curious about the subject of consciousness, wondering whether we humans have consciousness, and, if so, what the fundamental essence of consciousness is and what role it plays in the natural world.

To investigate the problem, I built a tiny transformer, training a small neural network to track four agents, four objects, and four locations through ordered events and determine who currently carries a queried object; for I reasoned that although the transformer is not identical to humans and its reasoning system is infinitely simplified, it does possess striking differences. When the transformer operates and attempts to answer the questions, like humans, it is undergoing the process of trying to extract the absolute laws of the world through empirical data (its training set), making predictions, and adjusting its expectations and reasoning according to the results and accuracy of those predictions.

After studying the mathematical principles behind the tiny transformer and linking those mathematical functions that operate the transformer to behaviors of our human bodies, I realized that though my results are plentiful, I had been lingering on the soft problem, the problem of the psychological mind, instead of the hard problem, the problem of consciousness.

I proceeded upon a line of philosophical inquiry, employing the use of self-introspection and logical, analytic reasoning, seemingly to discern four elementary attributes of consciousness. I also propose as a hypothesis that consciousness may play a role in the determination of causation by being the hidden factor in quantum mechanics.

In the whole essay, when I speak of consciousness, it should be taken as phenomenal consciousness.

\## Part I: The Soft Problem, or the Human Brain Is Just a Prediction Machine

“The brain is a prediction engine: it is constantly generating hypotheses about the world and testing them against incoming information.”

The mechanics of the brain, or our whole being, could roughly be divided into three categories, namely, the intrinsic hardware and natural structure of the brain, the information processing of the brain in response to sensory perceptions, and the learning process of the brain.

\### I.I Hardware of the Brain

The human brain is an intricately wired machine where the layers of information processing, natural preferences, and tendencies are all predetermined through the long process of natural selection and evolution. This is similar to the hard-coded network architecture of the transformer.

For the human mind, there are three layers. The first layer involves the basic functions of biology, such as breathing, shivering, and regulation of blood sugar. The second layer involves the fluctuation of primitive emotions such as aggression, voluptuous attraction, repulsion, exhilaration, and fear. The third layer is the gleaming beacon of sophistication in the human mind and concerns abstract reasoning, analytical judgment, and sensory decoding.

Genes, epigenetics, and the prenatal environment are the biological equivalents of base-weight initialization in transformers, for they constitute the primitive state in which you are equipped to face the novelties and perceptions of the outside world, and they determine your baseline personality, stress tolerance, and willpower. The changes and growth you subsequently experience are all adjustments and modifications upon those biological statistics.

Different people have different weight initializations as a result of luck, different genes, and different social environments.

\### I.II How the Brain Processes Data

Our human brain is constantly flooded with information, or, in the language of transformers, input tokens. There are three different kinds of tokens, each triggering a different kind of neural activity, specifically, sensory perception, the internal biological condition of our bodies, and lingering memories that are still active.

The brain unconsciously processes all kinds of different external information. For instance, when a person flashes before your eyes a photo of a person who is from a different racial group for a duration that is less than a second, your brain has already classified the racial token through the amygdala before you have consciously identified the photo.

The body generates numerous tokens of its own, heavily influencing the behavior and capacities of the mind. For instance, the frontal cortex requires great energy to conduct logical reasoning, so a low blood glucose level could impair judgment. Something that you have witnessed earlier might still be vibrating in a memory loop in your mind, influencing your present decisions.

Counterintuitively, the brain does not simply register a full, complex image of the external world; rather, it deploys multi-layer perceptrons. When the light hits your eyes, the visual cortex only perceives the basic features of light, whether the light is horizontal or straight, for instance. The next layer combines the various lines into recognizable geometric shapes. The subsequent layer assembles the shapes into a highly complex mental concept. The final layer passes this abstract concept on to the prefrontal cortex to decide on a final action.

Human cognition possesses its own attention heads, allowing us to examine two aspects of the problem when making a decision. For human beings, the dlPFC (Dorsolateral PFC) Attention Head and vmPFC (Ventromedial PFC) Attention Head run in parallel, with the dlPFC analyzing the situation in a cold, analytical, rational way, and the vmPFC “feeling” the situation, providing the emotional response to an action.

When you are making a moral decision, both attention heads contribute. Unexpectedly, if a person suffers damage to their vmPFC, they do not become logical, unfeeling robots; instead, they become paralyzed in everyday decision-making, for they lack the tiny emotional nudge towards making a specific decision.

To help us to be able to make swift decisions when confronted with potential dangers, the human body employs what is analogous to the residual connections in the transformer. The residual connection adds the new processed information onto the original information.

Instead of being subjected to the slow analysis of the visual cortex, which functions like a deep neural network, the sensory perception is directly delivered to the amygdala. In this case, the brain prioritizes speed over accuracy, preferring to, for instance, jump back from the shadow of a coiled rope that the amygdala had mistaken for a snake rather than take the risk of not jumping back from a snake because the visual cortex is still slowly identifying the image.

Neurobiology shows that the human brain employs the same self-attention mechanism as transformers when deciding whether a situation is dangerous or not. In a simple sketch, what the brain does is that it posits the Q (query)—is there danger?—calculates the K (key)—what does, say, this shadowy coiled shape represent?—and obtains the V (value)—it is dangerous, run, or it is safe, breathe slowly.

\### I.III How the Brain Learns

The human body possesses its own dynamic attention weights, where the meaning and significance of different tokens are assigned different values under different contexts. Stress and hormones, though they are not the causes of the actions, act as weights to amplify and diminish the tendency towards the actions.

Testosterone causes us to be more susceptible to threats, emphasizing aggression within us. Oxytocin, as the universal love hormone, dials up empathy towards people whom we classify as “us” and hostility towards those whom we classify as “they.” Glucocorticoids, meanwhile, encourage quick decisions, shutting down the slow, rational process of the frontal cortex during periods of stress.

The biological loss function is determined by stress levels and physical pain. Meanwhile, the dopaminergic reward prediction error system in our body performs the function of gradient descent in the transformer.

Dopamine does not just register the absolute value of pleasure. Instead, it discerns the discrepancy between the expected outcome and the real result. When you receive a better result than what you had previously expected, dopamine fires; when the outcome matches your expectations, dopamine remains at its constant level; inversely, when the outcome falls below what you had predicted, the dopamine level drops. The rise and fall of dopamine help the brain to adjust its expectations and behaviors so our next performance will be more correctly calibrated.

Active inference posits the same idea, though in different terminology, describing how the brain makes a prediction, matches it against the perception, and calculates the loss function using Variational Free Energy, which allows the brain to determine the loss without complex math. When there is a discrepancy between predictions and perceptions, the human body either updates its predictions or performs an action so the perception can better conform to the prediction.

There are two kinds of training corpora for human development. One is the historical training corpus, and the other is the personal training corpus. The resulting historical training corpus is rendered available to us through genes, accumulating centuries of ecology and culture, influencing the activity of our hormones.

The personal training corpus refers to the exterior environment. When we are born, our brain is highly immature, and our prefrontal cortex takes approximately the first 25 years of our lives to finish its open training run, which is almost entirely independent of the effects of genes.

\## Part II: The Hard Problem, or a Hypothesis on Consciousness

\### II.I What Is Consciousness?

Before discussions of consciousness can commence, it seems to me that it is necessary to first establish what consciousness exactly is. Yet since consciousness is, at this stage, still an abstract concept that we cannot confidently map onto a certain mathematical concept or equation and discover, irrefutably, properties X, Y, and Z, all the following attributes will be obtained from introspection and empirical observation.

In my opinion, when contemplating the concept of consciousness, one of the most crucial steps is not to confuse consciousness, or phenomenal consciousness, with awareness, or what some may call psychological consciousness.

Awareness is characterized by what a mind does, specifically, its causal role. The previous discussion on the soft problem is, in other words, a discussion of awareness, or psychological consciousness. Consciousness, on the other hand, refers to the what-it-feels-like experience and serves no functional or causal roles.

Some philosophers and neuroscientists contend that there is no consciousness and that it is identical to and can be reduced to neuron firing. I disagree with this viewpoint because we can directly access consciousness, and it is futile to deny its existence. Perhaps we can in no way detect gravity, but still it would be erroneous to reduce it to matter and energy.

\### II.II The Attributes of Consciousness

This essay contends that consciousness possesses four important attributes. The attributes of being ubiquitous, fundamental, and united are universal, while the attribute of self-interpretation is one that applies to only certain kinds of consciousness.

Consciousness is fundamental; it is not a composite of different substances. Its existence is similar to the existence of gravity, necessarily entailed by the laws of nature. It is in itself nonphysical, but its existence is dependent on the existence of physical substances, just as gravity is dependent on mass or energy without itself being an object of mass or a physical object capable of being detected in particles and waves.

Consciousness is ubiquitous. This proposition is appealing because it solves the problem of how nonphysical substance emerges from physical substance. David Chalmers argues for panpsychism by saying that information is ubiquitous, and experience is associated with information. Thus, experience is ubiquitous.

It is widely believed that cats and dogs have consciousness, though less complex than humans. If it is contended that only biological creatures can possess consciousness, then it inevitably leads to the idea that consciousness is triggered by the presence of neurons, which raises the question of why neurons specifically trigger consciousness and what is special about neurons.

The theory that everything entertains consciousness seems to be a simpler and more defensible position despite its counterintuitiveness, for it removes the special mystery around neurons, which seem intrinsically no different in composition from other cells. Analogously, it is as if we have abandoned the idea that only planets have gravity and instead adopted the fact that every mass and energy innately causes gravity. In this case, a thermostat also has consciousness, though likewise less complex than animals.

In response to the objection that if all information is necessarily accompanied by consciousness, then why only a small amount of the information in human cognitive processing seems to correspond to the information in conscious experience, the answer is simple. The other information that we do not consciously experience is not missing; rather, it is experienced by neurons or other particles and cells or even electrons of my body. In other words, “unconscious” information, though not realized in my experience, is realized in the experience of one of my neurons in the way that experience is associated with a thermostat.

Through access to our own consciousness, we observe that our consciousness maintains unity as one self-subject. The unity itself isn't particularly worth discussion; it is just a fundamental attribute of consciousness that we can only take for granted, as we take for granted that a triangle has three angles adding up to 180 degrees.

What is a topic of interest, however, is how this unity came into existence. It is suggested that unity might possibly emerge through synthesized oscillation, for humans at least, where the oscillations are able to provide the neural network with a connection and interaction strong enough for a whole new consciousness to arise from the individual consciousnesses of particles.

The theory that higher-order unified consciousness possibly emerges when a certain level of connection and interaction is reached receives support from the study of human neurobiology. In the human brain, the cerebrum, whose parts interact with each other more, is more related to conscious experience even though the cerebellum has more neurons.

Physics naturally allows mental states and consciousness to fuse into one singular unity. This is analogous to the fact that when objects fall into a black hole, they don't just sit next to each other; they lose their individual “ID cards” and are crushed into a single, new point. (William Seager)

Human consciousness places an emphasis upon self-interpretation. In our stream of consciousness, two kinds of consciousness can be found, namely, consciousness of sensory perception and consciousness of thought.

In our daily life, it is not uncommon that we feel a spike of emotion whose origin we cannot identify. Similarly, we can entertain theories about our own feelings and later develop new theories and interpretations of the same feelings.

We are not guaranteed knowledge and insight into ourselves, or our functional selves, the part of us that is almost irrelevant to our phenomenal experience and is dominated and characterized by the causal role it plays. This suggests that awareness and consciousness are two distinct identities.

Awareness is not always accompanied by consciousness, for we have unconscious beliefs and behaviors. Conversely, what is presented in consciousness does not necessarily need to be present in awareness. The contents of consciousness do, however, stem from the basis of awareness.

Chalmers's zombie thought experiment is, to an extent, supported by the split-brain experiment, which seems to support the distinction and individuality between consciousness and awareness, reinforcing the credibility of Chalmers's zombie thought experiment.

The split-brain experiment is conducted on people who have suffered damage to the connection between the left hemisphere and right hemisphere of their brain. The right hemisphere of the brain receives information from our left visual field, while the left hemisphere of the brain has access to the perceptions of our right visual field.

In almost all right-handed people, the capacity for speech, writing, and language is almost entirely concentrated in the left hemisphere. The right hemisphere is non-speaking; it can comprehend simple spoken words to a limited extent, but it cannot produce speech.

The experiment is conducted by flashing images very quickly on two sides. The abruptness of the appearance and disappearance of the images eliminates the eye's ability to relocate and ensures that the image only appears in its specific, designated visual field.

During the experiment, when an image flashes across the right, the patient names the object shown; when the image is flashed across the left, however, the patient responds that they see nothing. Yet when they are told to use their left hand, which is controlled by the right hemisphere, to pick out the image that they had just seen, they are able to pick up the right object, and when they are asked to explain this, they give a wrong, invented answer, an attempt by the left hemisphere to explain the phenomenon.

The right-hand side still receives and processes information, but there is no way that it could communicate that to the outer world, illustrating the disparity between what we perceive to be a person's inner world and their true state.

Although this experiment does not necessarily prove the existence of Chalmers's philosophical zombie, it does make it seem more plausible. In the patient, if consciousness existed as a whole for the connection of the two brains, then there is a distinction between consciousness and what is reported, as what is reported is limited to the left-hand side of the brain, and consciousness being in accordance with what is reported would imply that the cause of consciousness is located somewhere in the left-hand side of the brain.

If there is insufficient neural activity to breed consciousness, then the patient is a member of Chalmers's philosophical zombie.

\## Conclusion: Consciousness and Quantum Mechanics

This essay has previously explored the basic essence of both the phenomenal mind and the psychological mind, but it leaves out the question of precisely why the existence of consciousness is necessary.

This essay will thus now review the theories in which it is proposed that consciousness is involved in transforming the various superposition states in quantum mechanics into a definite outcome that we observe.

Wigner-von claims that consciousness turns states in quantum mechanics from superposition to a definite branch. Wigner's friend argues that when your friend is running a quantum experiment with a definite outcome, such as a flash or no flash, in the other room, you do not know the results until you have heard them, at which point the wave function collapses, but your friend has already seen the results prior to you in the room.

This essay suggests that consciousness acts as the hidden factor in quantum mechanics, where the hidden factor does not have locality. Consciousness selects the possibility of the quantum mechanism.

Since consciousness is ubiquitous, it escapes the criticism that there is an instance and spot where the wave function does not collapse, since every physical substance entails consciousness.
